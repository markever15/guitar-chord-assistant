How to use the Chord Finder

The Chord Finder works backwards from the usual chord chart. You already have your fingers somewhere on the neck and you want to know what it is called — so tap that shape onto the fretboard above and it will name it for you.

  1. Tap the frets you are holding. One tap per string. Tap the same spot again to clear it.
  2. Set the strings you are not playing. The O and X markers down the left side switch each string between ringing open and muted.
  3. Read the result. The panel names the chord and lists the notes you are actually sounding. Show Notes marks where else those notes live on the neck, and Play sounds the shape back so you can check it by ear.

If nothing comes back, the shape usually has a note in it that no standard chord contains — mute a string and try again. Stuck? Example drops in a shape to start from.

Looking up a chord you already know the name of

That is the other direction, and it lives in the Chord Dictionary tab: pick a root, pick a quality, and you get every playable voicing with the fingering marked on each diagram. Shapes that leave out a note are labelled, so you always know what you are hearing.

You can also browse the whole dictionary as plain pages — every chord grouped by root and family, no tapping required.

Why the voicings change

Pick the same four chords twice and you may get different shapes. That is the point: this picks the set where your fingers move least between one chord and the next, rather than the shapes you happened to learn first.

The top note line underneath matters more than it looks. The highest string is the one an ear follows as a melody, so a progression whose top note steps by a tone or two sounds connected, while one that leaps sounds like four separate chords.

Switch Open chords off when you want everything in one position further up the neck — useful with a band, or when a capo is already taking the open strings.

Beyond plain triads

Three-note chords are where a key starts, not where it stops. Chord flavour restacks every degree at once: sevenths for jazz and soul, add9 for something open and modern, sixths when a seventh sounds too final, sus when you want to sit between major and minor.

The degrees are not interchangeable, so the flavour adapts. In a major key the V becomes a dominant seventh while the I and IV become major sevenths — that difference is what makes the V pull home. Where a flavour would drag in a note from outside the key, that chord stays a triad.

Tap any chord in your progression to change just that one. Most songs are not one flavour all the way through; a single m7 in a row of triads is often the whole idea.

What this site does

Most chord charts hand you one diagram per chord and leave it there. That is fine for an open G, but it falls apart the moment you need a Cmaj7add11 in the middle of a song and the only shape you can find needs six frets of reach. This site takes the opposite approach: it lists every voicing that is actually playable, shows the fingering for each one, and marks which notes are missing when a shape leaves one out.

The chord dictionary

Pick a root and a chord quality and you get the full set of shapes for it, sorted from the nut upward. Each diagram carries a finger number on every dot, an X over strings you mute, and an O over strings you let ring. Where a thumb-over grip is the sensible way to hold a shape, the diagram says T instead of a finger number rather than pretending your index can be in two places at once.

Extended chords rarely fit all their notes on six strings, so shapes that drop a note are grouped separately and labelled with the degree they omit — 5th omitted, for instance. Nothing is filed under a chord name it does not earn: a voicing is only listed if the notes left ringing still spell that chord and nothing simpler. Drop the 5th from a 6/9 and what remains is a 7sus2 a whole step up, so those shapes are left out entirely rather than filed under the wrong name.

The chord finder

The Chord Finder runs the other direction. Tap the fretboard to build the shape your fingers are already holding and it tells you what the chord is called, which notes are sounding, and what else that grip could be read as. It is the fastest way to name something you stumbled onto, or to check whether the shape you half-remember from a video is the chord you think it is.

Slash chords and inversions

Chords with a bass note other than the root get their own section under each chord. These are the shapes that make a progression walk downward instead of jumping around, and they are listed only where the alternate bass adds something the plain voicing cannot.

Where to start

If you are new to reading these diagrams, the fretboard diagram guide covers the notation in a few minutes. From there, essential chord progressions gives you something to play, and barre chord tips deals with the shapes that stop most players in their first year. The Tips tab has fifteen articles in all, covering capos, transposing, voice leading, and the extended chords this dictionary spends most of its time on.
