15. Practice Drills for Fast, Clean Chord Changes

Playing whole songs is enjoyable and it is the slowest way to fix a specific bad chord change. If one change in a song is letting you down, you get maybe eight attempts at it per run-through, separated by two minutes of playing things you can already do. Drills remove the two minutes.

The two-chord loop

The core drill, and the one that fixes most problems.

  1. Pick the two chords you actually struggle with. Not the whole song — the one change.
  2. Set a metronome slow enough that you never miss. Genuinely slow. If you are rushing, it is too fast.
  3. One strum per chord, one bar each. Change on the beat, every time.
  4. Only raise the tempo after four clean repetitions in a row.

Two minutes of this is worth more than twenty minutes of playing the song. You are training one specific movement, and movements improve with repetition close together in time.

Form the shape in the air first

Watch someone with a sloppy change and you will usually see their fingers land one at a time and then shuffle into position. The fix is to build the whole shape before it touches the strings.

Practise it deliberately: hold a chord, lift your hand a couple of centimetres off the neck, form the next shape in mid-air, then place all the fingers down together. Slow, exaggerated, no strumming. After a few minutes your hand starts arriving pre-formed, which is the actual skill.

Find the anchor finger

Many chord pairs share a finger position, and moving one that did not need to move costs time and accuracy.

  • C to Am — index and middle stay exactly where they are. Only the ring finger moves.
  • Em to Am — the middle and ring fingers keep their shape, shifted one string over.
  • G to Em — two fingers can stay planted depending on your G fingering.

Before drilling a change, put both chords down and look for a finger that could stay. If one can, keeping it planted also guarantees the shapes land in the right place relative to each other.

Keep strumming through the change

A common trap: practising changes with the strumming hand stopped, then finding the change falls apart when the rhythm comes back. Real playing does not pause for your left hand.

So drill it the honest way. Keep the strumming hand moving in steady quarter notes and change chords on beat one whether or not you are ready. You will produce some ugly noise on the beat you arrive late. That noise is the information you need, and it disappears faster when you can hear exactly where it happens.

Where drills belong in a session

Early, and briefly. Fifteen minutes of focused drilling at the start of practice, while you are fresh, does more than an hour tacked on at the end. Then play music.

Rotate what you drill. Working the same change for a week produces diminishing returns; three changes rotated over a fortnight will all improve. And drop a drill once it is clean — the goal is to stop needing it.

How to know it worked

Not by whether it feels easier — that is unreliable. Record thirty seconds of the change at a fixed tempo, note the tempo, and record it again a week later. Either it is cleaner at that tempo or it is not. Most players find the improvement is larger than it felt, which is worth knowing when a change seems to be going nowhere.
