14. Extended Chords in Pop & R&B: 9ths, 11ths, and 13ths

Extended chords keep stacking thirds past the 7th. A 9th is the 2nd an octave up, an 11th is the 4th an octave up, a 13th is the 6th an octave up. Structurally they are the 7th chords you already know with more on top.

ChordNotesWhat it adds
C7C E G Bbthe starting point
C9C E G Bb Da 9th on top
C11C E G Bb D Fa 9th and an 11th
C13C E G Bb D Aa 9th and a 13th

You cannot play all of them

C13 has six notes and you have six strings, but they are not spread the way a hand can reach. In practice players drop notes, and there is a standard order for what goes first:

  1. The 5th goes first. It adds almost nothing that the root has not already implied.
  2. The root can go too, if a bass player is covering it.
  3. The 11th usually goes in a dominant chord, because it clashes badly with the major 3rd.

What must stay is the 3rd, the 7th, and whatever extension gives the chord its name. Those three notes are the chord's identity; everything else is padding. This is why jazz guitarists play so many three- and four-note voicings — not to sound sparse, but because those are the notes that matter.

add9 is not 9

A common trap. Cadd9 is C–E–G–D: a plain major triad with a 9th added, no 7th. C9 is C–E–G–Bb–D: a dominant 7th chord with a 9th.

The difference is not subtle. Cadd9 is open and bright and belongs in folk and rock — it is what an ordinary C sounds like with the D string ringing. C9 is a dominant chord with tension that wants to resolve. Substituting one for the other will change what the progression does.

Where they turn up

maj9 and maj7 in place of plain major chords. Swap the C in a progression for Cmaj9 and it goes from stated to floating. This is most of what makes neo-soul and quiet-storm R&B sound the way it does.

m9 and m11 in place of minor chords. Am9 is a much richer thing than Am and often costs no extra effort to reach.

9 and 13 on dominant chords. A funk rhythm part is often just one 9th chord with the right hand doing all the work. James Brown records live on this.

7#9 when you want dirt. The famous Hendrix chord — the raised 9th grinds against the major 3rd on purpose.

Using them without wrecking a song

  • Extend one chord, not all of them. Extensions work as contrast. Applied to every chord they cancel out and the progression turns to soup.
  • Keep the function. Cmaj9 still behaves as C. Extensions change colour, not the job the chord does.
  • Watch the register. Extensions stacked low sound muddy. That is why these voicings usually live above the 5th fret.
  • Leave room for the vocal. A dense chord under a melody fights it. If it sounds cluttered, drop a note rather than moving the chord.

A place to start

Take a progression you already play and change exactly one chord. C–Am–F–G becomes C–Am–Fmaj7–G, or C–Am9–F–G. Look the new chord up in the dictionary, take the voicing closest to where your hand already is, and listen to what changed. One chord at a time is how these become part of your playing rather than a section of a theory book.
