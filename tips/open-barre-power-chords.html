8. Open, Barre, and Power Chords — When to Use Each

These three get taught as a difficulty ladder, and that is misleading. Experienced players do not choose the hardest one available — they choose the one that suits the song. Each has a genuinely different sound and a different job.

Open chords

Shapes using open strings near the nut: C, G, D, A, E and their minors. They ring longer and brighter than anything else on the instrument, because open strings sustain more than fretted ones and are not damped by a finger.

Use them for anything acoustic, anything with space in the arrangement, anything where you want the guitar to sound like a guitar rather than a block of harmony.

The catch is keys. Open shapes exist comfortably in about six keys. Songs in Bb, Eb, or F# will not accommodate them without a capo — which is precisely what a capo is for.

Barre chords

Movable six-string shapes. Learn the E shape and the A shape and you can play any major, minor, or 7th chord anywhere on the neck.

Use them for keys open chords cannot reach, for a consistent full-bodied sound across a progression, and for anything where you need to cut a chord off cleanly — releasing the pressure silences every string at once, which open chords cannot do.

The catch is that they are uniform. Six strings of even sustain is exactly right in some arrangements and heavy-handed in others. Barre everything and a song can start to sound like a wall.

Power chords

Root and 5th only, no 3rd, written with a 5 — C5, G5. Two or three strings, one small shape, movable anywhere.

Use them for distorted rock and metal. The reason is physical: with heavy gain, the intervals in a full chord produce intermodulation that muddies the sound, while a bare fifth stays clean and defined however hard you drive it.

The other advantage is that with no 3rd, a power chord is neither major nor minor. One shape covers both, which is why riffs move around the neck so freely.

The catch is that they carry no harmonic information. On a clean tone with no bass underneath, a progression of power chords sounds hollow and unresolved.

Choosing in practice

SituationReach for
Solo acoustic, singing over itOpen chords, capo if needed
Song in Bb or EbBarre, or capo plus open
Distorted riffPower chords
Second guitar under another partPartial barre or three-string voicing
Chord stabs that must stop deadBarre
FingerpickingOpen, or a voicing with open strings in it

Mixing them in one song

Most real parts do. A common approach is open chords in the verse for air and detail, barre chords in the chorus for weight, and power chords for whatever needs to hit hard. The change in texture does as much work as the change in volume.

The Chord Dictionary lists every voicing for a chord side by side, from open position to the top of the neck, so you can hear the same chord in each of these forms and pick by sound. Two voicings of the same chord can sit completely differently in an arrangement, and choosing between them is where a guitar part starts to sound arranged rather than merely played.

Partial voicings, the fourth option

Between a full barre and a power chord there is a large space that gets taught rarely and used constantly: three- and four-string voicings taken from the middle of a barre shape.

Take the E-shape barre for F. Play only the top four strings — x x 3 2 1 1 — and you have the whole chord with none of the effort. Play only the middle strings and you have something that sits above a bass guitar without competing with it. Neither is a compromise; both are what session players reach for when a full six-string chord would be too much.

This matters most when there is another instrument in the arrangement. Two guitars both playing full barre chords fight each other. One playing open shapes low and the other playing three-string voicings higher up sounds like an arrangement.

Learn them in this order

Open chords first, because they let you play actual songs immediately. Then power chords, which are easy and open up a huge amount of rock repertoire. Barre chords last, because they are the hardest and the least fun to practise — but do not put them off indefinitely, because until you have them, every song in an awkward key stays out of reach.
