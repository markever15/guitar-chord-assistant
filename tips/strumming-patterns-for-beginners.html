11. Strumming Patterns Every Beginner Should Know

Chord shapes get you halfway. The strumming hand is what turns a set of chords into music, and it is usually the hand that gets no deliberate practice at all — most people spend their attention on the fretting hand and let the right hand do whatever it happens to do.

Keep the hand moving

Before any pattern, the underlying habit: your strumming hand should move continuously up and down, like a metronome, whether or not it hits the strings. A pattern is not a series of separate movements — it is a constant motion where some passes make contact and some do not.

This is the single biggest difference between playing that feels stiff and playing that feels natural. If you stop your hand to skip a strum, the timing goes with it.

The patterns worth knowing

All downs. D D D D, one per beat. Boring and essential. Play a whole song this way and concentrate on landing exactly with the click.

Down, down-up. D DU D DU. Your first pattern with any groove in it. The hand keeps moving through the missing upstroke.

The common one. D DU UDU — down, down-up, up-down-up. Written out it looks complicated; in the hand it is just eight continuous motions with the third and sixth missing the strings. This pattern will carry an enormous number of pop, folk, and rock songs.

The sixteenth-note funk one. Twice the speed, hand moving constantly, hitting whichever strokes the groove asks for. This is where muting becomes essential.

Muting

Two different techniques, both worth having.

Fretting-hand mute. Relax your fingers so they rest on the strings without pressing to the fret. Strum and you get a percussive chunk instead of a chord. Drop these in on the backbeat and a flat pattern turns into a groove. It is also a way to keep the pattern going while you change chords — the mute covers the gap.

Palm mute. Rest the edge of your strumming hand lightly on the strings near the bridge. Notes still sound but shortened and tight. Essential for rock, and the basis of most riff playing.

Dynamics matter more than the pattern

A guitarist playing all downstrokes with real dynamics sounds better than one playing a complicated pattern at one flat volume. Accent beats 2 and 4 and almost anything starts to feel like music.

Where you strum changes the sound too. Over the soundhole is warm and full; nearer the bridge is thin and bright and cuts through. Moving between them within a song is free arrangement.

How to practise it

  1. Put on a metronome at a slow tempo.
  2. Mute all the strings with your fretting hand — no chord, just percussion. Play the pattern until it is automatic.
  3. Add one chord. Just one, held down, nothing changing.
  4. Add a second chord and change every bar.

Separating the hands is the point. Trying to learn a new pattern and a chord change simultaneously means neither gets your full attention, and the pattern is usually what quietly gets abandoned.

Copying a song

Do not try to transcribe the pattern note for note. Listen for where the accents fall and match those first; the small details between them matter far less than you would think. Get the accents right and it sounds like the song, even when the details differ.

Counting it out loud

If a pattern will not stick, the problem is usually that you do not know where you are in the bar. Count aloud — "one and two and three and four and" — with downstrokes on the numbers and upstrokes on the ands. Now any pattern can be written down: D DU UDU is down on 1, down on 2, up on the and of 2, up on the and of 3, down on 4, up on the and of 4.

Saying it aloud while you play feels ridiculous and works better than anything else. It forces the pattern to line up with the bar instead of floating loose.

Common problems

  • Speeding up. Nearly universal, and nearly always at the point where the strumming gets busier. A metronome catches it; playing alone does not.
  • Hitting too many strings. On chords with muted low strings you do not need to strum all six. Aim at the middle of the chord and let the edges look after themselves.
  • Gripping the pick too hard. A tight grip makes the pick dig in and the tone harsh. Hold it just firmly enough that it does not fall.
  • Stopping between chords. Back to the first rule — keep the hand moving. Mute through the change if you need to.
