4. Slash Chords Explained

A chord written X/Y means "play chord X, but put note Y in the bass." That is the whole rule. It looks like division and it is not — it is a chord you probably already know, with an instruction about which note goes on the bottom.

Two different things wear the same notation

If Y is already in the chord, you have an inversion. C/E and C/G are both still C major — same three notes, different one at the bottom. Nothing has been added; the furniture has been rearranged.

If Y is not in the chord, something has been added. Am/G is an A minor triad with a G underneath, and A–C–E–G is exactly Am7. The slash spelling is used when the writer wants that G specifically in the bass rather than anywhere in the voicing. C/D, C/B, and G/A work the same way.

Knowing which kind you are looking at tells you how much freedom you have. With an inversion you can voice the chord however you like as long as the right note is lowest. With an added bass note, moving it somewhere else changes the chord.

Why anyone bothers

Because the bass line is what makes a progression feel like it is moving. Compare these:

  • C – Am – F — bass notes C, A, F. Every change is a leap. The progression works, but it sits still.
  • C – G/B – Am — bass notes C, B, A. A stepwise walk down. Same harmony, completely different sense of motion.

That descending bass is one of the most reused devices in popular music. Extend it and you get C – G/B – Am – Am/G – F, a bass line walking C–B–A–G–F while the chords above barely change. Whole verses have been built on nothing else.

It works upward too. F – G/F – C/E – Dm gives a bass that steps F–F–E–D under chords that are moving in a different direction, which is the sort of tension arrangers use to keep a slow section from sagging.

Playing them on guitar

This is where guitar differs from piano. A pianist just plays the bass note with the left hand. On guitar the lowest string you actually strum becomes the bass, so a slash chord usually means not playing a string you would normally include.

For C/E you leave the low E string ringing and skip the A string root. For C/G you fret the 3rd fret of the low E. For Am/G you fret the 3rd fret of the low E under an ordinary A minor shape. In every case the change is on the bass side of the chord, and the top of the shape barely moves.

The slash chords listed under each chord in the Chord Dictionary already account for this — the strings marked X are the ones that would put the wrong note on the bottom, so the fingering shown is playable as written rather than a theoretical spelling you have to adapt.

Reading them in a chart

Two things to watch for when you meet one in the wild.

The bass note may already be there. If the previous chord left your finger on the note you need, the "new" chord might be one finger's difference. Charts rarely point this out.

In a band, you may not need it at all. A slash chord is an instruction about the lowest sounding note, and if a bass player is covering that note, playing the plain chord above them produces the intended sound. Where it genuinely matters is solo playing, where you are the bass line as well as the harmony.

Try it

Play C – Am – F – G a few times and listen to the bass jumping around. Then play C – G/B – Am – F, changing nothing but that second chord. The harmony has hardly moved, and the progression suddenly goes somewhere. That one substitution is the cheapest trick in songwriting, which is why it is everywhere once you start listening for it.
