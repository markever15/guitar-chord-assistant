5. Essential Chord Progressions to Practice

A surprising share of popular music runs on a handful of chord progressions, reused across genres and decades. Learn these by feel and you will start hearing where a song is going before it gets there — and you will write your own faster, because you are choosing between known shapes instead of guessing chord by chord.

Reading the numerals

Progressions are written in roman numerals because the pattern matters more than the letters. I is the chord built on the first note of the key, V the chord on the fifth note, and so on. Uppercase means major, lowercase means minor. That is why I–V–vi–IV describes the same progression in every key, even though the letter names change completely.

The five worth knowing

I–IV–V — C–F–G. The backbone of blues, rock, and country. Three major chords, maximum resolution, no ambiguity. Almost every twelve-bar blues is this and nothing else.

I–V–vi–IV — C–G–Am–F. The one people mean when they joke about four-chord songs. It works because it starts resolved, dips into the relative minor, then walks home.

vi–IV–I–V — Am–F–C–G. The same four chords rotated to start on the minor. Identical harmony, noticeably darker opening, which is why it turns up in choruses that need to sound bigger than the verse.

ii–V–I — Dm7–G7–Cmaj7. The core jazz cadence and the strongest sense of arrival in tonal music. The 7ths matter here; play it as plain triads and most of the effect disappears.

I–vi–IV–V — C–Am–F–G. The 1950s doo-wop turnaround. Instantly period-specific, which makes it either a gift or a trap depending on what you are writing.

Working out a song by ear

Find the home chord first. Play along and look for the one the song could end on without sounding unfinished — that is your I. Once you have it, the rest usually falls into place, because most songs draw from the seven chords of that key and the patterns above cover the common routes between them.

If a chord refuses to fit, the two usual suspects are a borrowed major V in a minor key, or a bass note that has made an ordinary chord look unfamiliar. The Chord Finder settles the second case quickly.

Making a known progression sound new

Once a progression is under your fingers, the interesting work is in the voicings rather than the chords.

  • Change the bass. C–G–Am–F becomes C–G/B–Am–F, and the bass walks C–B–A instead of jumping. Same chords, completely different motion.
  • Add a 7th. Swapping C for Cmaj7 or G for G7 changes the colour without changing the function. A maj7 softens, a dominant 7th pushes forward.
  • Move up the neck. Playing the same progression at the 5th or 8th fret changes which register the chords sit in, which matters enormously when there is a vocal on top.
  • Keep a note ringing. Find a voicing of each chord that shares a top note and the progression starts to sound deliberate rather than assembled.

Loop one progression slowly with a metronome and substitute a single chord each time round. You will learn more about how harmony actually feels in ten minutes of that than in an hour of reading about it.

Where the emotion actually comes from

These progressions are not interchangeable, and the difference is where they start and end rather than which chords they contain. I–V–vi–IV and vi–IV–I–V use exactly the same four chords in the same order — the second one just starts a step later in the loop. Yet the first opens bright and settled and the second opens dark, because the chord you begin on becomes the reference point for everything that follows.

The same applies to endings. A progression finishing on V feels unfinished and wants to loop again, which is why so many verses end there. One finishing on I feels closed, which is why choruses often do.

Twelve-bar blues

Worth knowing on its own because so much rock and roll is built directly on it. In C, twelve bars of I–IV–V arranged as: four bars of C, two of F, two of C, one of G, one of F, one of C, one of G. Learn it once and you can play along with an enormous amount of music you have never heard before, in any key, by moving the same pattern.
